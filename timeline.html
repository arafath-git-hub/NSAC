01 THE JOURNEY THROUGH TIME

A HISTORY WRITTEN IN MACHINES

Missions end.
Their traces remain.

Every mission has a beginning, a purpose, and an ending. But on the Moon and Mars, the end of a mission does not necessarily mean the end of its physical presence.

Landing stages, scientific instruments, rovers, and other equipment remain at distant locations long after their original tasks are complete. This timeline follows some of the missions that created those lasting traces.

1969 THE FIRST FOOTPRINTS
THE MOON / USA

Apollo 11

On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 landed in the Sea of Tranquility. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the lunar surface.

After the astronauts returned to lunar orbit, the Lunar Module's descent stage remained at the landing site. It became one of the earliest human-made objects deliberately left on the Moon.

1970 ROBOTIC EXPLORATION
THE MOON / SOVIET UNION

Lunokhod 1

The Soviet Union's Lunokhod 1 became the first successful remote-controlled rover to operate on another world.

It travelled across the lunar surface, photographed its surroundings, and performed scientific measurements. Its mission ended in 1971, leaving the rover on the Moon.

1971 ANOTHER WORLD
MARS / SOVIET UNION

Mars 3

The Soviet Mars 3 mission achieved the first successful soft landing on Mars in December 1971.

Its lander began transmitting data from the surface, but communication stopped shortly afterward. The spacecraft's hardware remained on the Martian surface.

1972 THE FINAL APOLLO LANDING
THE MOON / USA

Apollo 17

Apollo 17 marked the final crewed lunar landing of the Apollo program. Astronauts Eugene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt explored the Taurus-Littrow valley.

The mission left behind a Lunar Module descent stage, scientific equipment, and other objects at the landing site.

1976 THE FIRST US LANDERS
MARS / USA

Viking 1 and Viking 2

NASA's Viking landers reached Mars in 1976. They returned the first successful US surface images from the planet.

Their scientific experiments studied Martian soil and atmosphere. Both landers eventually stopped operating, but their structures remained on Mars.

1997 THE FIRST MARTIAN ROVER
MARS / USA

Pathfinder and Sojourner

NASA's Mars Pathfinder mission landed on Mars on July 4, 1997. It deployed Sojourner, a small robotic rover that explored the surrounding terrain.

Pathfinder and Sojourner demonstrated a new approach to planetary exploration. The mission ended after communications were lost in September 1997.

Explore Sojourner ↗
2004 THE LONG JOURNEY
MARS / USA

Spirit and Opportunity

NASA's twin Mars Exploration Rovers arrived on Mars in January 2004. Spirit landed in Gusev Crater, while Opportunity landed in Meridiani Planum.

Both rovers greatly exceeded their original planned mission duration. Spirit's mission ended in 2010, while Opportunity's mission ended in 2018.

2012 A NEW GENERATION
MARS / USA

Curiosity arrives

NASA's Curiosity rover landed inside Gale Crater on August 6, 2012. Its mission was designed to investigate whether Mars once had environments capable of supporting microbial life.

Curiosity remains an active mission. It is not abandoned equipment; it represents the continuing chapter of robotic exploration.

2018 THE END OF AN ERA
MARS / USA

Opportunity's final communication

A planet-wide dust storm prevented Opportunity from receiving enough sunlight to recharge its batteries. The rover sent its last communication in June 2018.

NASA officially declared the mission complete in February 2019 after repeated attempts to re-establish contact. Opportunity remained on Mars.

2021 ANOTHER LANDING
MARS / USA

Perseverance and Ingenuity

NASA's Perseverance rover landed in Jezero Crater on February 18, 2021. Its mission includes searching for signs of ancient microbial life and collecting rock samples.

The Ingenuity helicopter demonstrated powered flight on another planet. Its flight operations ended in January 2024 after damage to its rotor blades.

Perseverance remains an active rover mission.

The next chapter

Exploration continues. Active missions, new scientific instruments, and future spacecraft will add new chapters to the history of human activity beyond Earth.

The objects left by earlier missions remain reminders of how far exploration has travelled — and how much remains unknown.
